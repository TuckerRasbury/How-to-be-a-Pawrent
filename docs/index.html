How This Works

What this is: A care guide for Sunny and Luna, built for anyone watching them — whether that's you at 7am or a dog sitter who just walked in the door. Everything you need is here. You don't need to install anything or know how to use an app.

Using the Today page

  1. Open the app. The What's Next block at the top tells you exactly what to do right now for each dog.
  2. Below that is the full day's schedule — both dogs, in order.
  3. Tap any schedule block to expand the full instructions for that task.
  4. Experienced caretakers: scan and skip. New caretakers: tap everything.

WFH / Office toggle

The small pill at the top of the screen says WFH or Office. Set it once in the morning based on whether the owner is home or away. It changes Luna's schedule — office days require midday coverage. When set to Office, a reminder banner appears at the top.

Reading a schedule block

Each block shows the time, which dog it's for, and the task. A NON-NEGOTIABLE badge means this task cannot be skipped or delayed — it affects Luna's training or Sunny's health directly.

Tap the block to see the full instructions.

Finding emergency info

There is a red 🚨 button in the bottom-right corner of every page. Tap it once for vet numbers, owner numbers, and what to do in specific situations. You can also reach it from the Emergency tab in the bottom navigation.

Who to call

Owner contacts are on the Emergency page. When in doubt, call. They would rather get a "just checking" text than a surprise.

A note about Luna

Luna is a young dog in active training. The protocols on her page — especially the biting protocol and crate schedule — are not suggestions. They are part of her development, and consistency across every caretaker is what makes them work. One person doing it differently sets back everyone's progress. Please follow them exactly.

Adding photos

  2. Drop it into the /images folder in the GitHub repo
  3. Push to GitHub — it appears automatically in the carousel, gallery, and Today page background

Commands & Games

How to ask, how to reward, and how to keep it sustainable

Say each command once, calmly and clearly. If they don't respond, reset — don't repeat. Repetition without response teaches them the word is optional. Mark the exact moment they do the right thing with "Yes!", then reward. Short, positive sessions beat long, exhausting ones every time.
Behavior Notes
Luna — Small Dog Reactivity ▾
What happened: Luna lunged hard at a small dog at ~10–15 feet. Once locked on, she was unresponsive to Sit and to leash correction. She stayed fixated on the direction the dog went even after she settled. Why this happens: Small, fast-moving animals are a stronger prey-drive trigger. Once she hits full sympathetic arousal — locked onto a stimulus — commands don't land. Her brain isn't available for them. This is the same mechanism as cat fixation, triggered specifically by tiny dogs. This is not aggression. It is hardwired prey drive in her breed mix. Impulse control around it can absolutely be built — but it takes structured work, not repeated walk encounters. On walks right now: • Identify small dogs from a distance before she does • Create space before she hits the lock-on threshold (~15 feet was the point of no return) • Redirecting her attention to you only works before that window closes — not after Long-term training: • Controlled intros with small dogs owned by friends, in low-arousal contexts (after exercise or a meal, at home) • Start at a distance where she can notice the dog without reacting — reward calm attention toward it • Repeat many times in structured settings before expecting reliability on walks • Counter-conditioning: pair the sight of small dogs with high-value treats to build a positive association Prognosis: Very trainable at her age. Requires intentional, structured exposure — not hoping walk encounters go well on their own.
Commands
Sit ▾
Say "Sit" once, calmly. If they need help: hold a treat at nose level and slowly move it back over their head. Their bottom will lower on its own. The instant their butt hits the ground → "Yes!" and reward. Don't repeat the word. If they don't respond within 3 seconds, reset and try again. Practice in calm spots before adding distractions.
Down ▾
Ask for "Down" once, from a Sit. Lure: hold the treat at their nose, lower it straight down to the floor between their paws, then slide it slightly toward you. They should fold into a down. The instant both elbows touch the floor → "Yes!" and reward. Never push the dog down physically. The lure builds the behavior without pressure or stress.
Touch ▾
Hold your flat palm out about 6 inches from their nose. Say "Touch" and wait. The moment their nose makes contact with your palm → "Yes!" and reward. Touch is one of the most useful tools you have. It redirects attention, supports recall, builds focus, and gives you a way to guide the dog without grabbing them. Build distance and duration gradually once they get it reliably up close.
Paw ▾
Ask for a Sit first. Once she's sitting, extend your hand like a handshake — palm facing up, fingers slightly curled. Say "Paw" and wait. If she lifts her paw and reaches toward your hand → open your hand and let her find the treat inside. Mark with "Yes!" as her paw makes contact. Once she's getting it consistently, raise the difficulty: present an empty hand the same way. When she paws it → "Yes!" and deliver the treat from your other hand or your pocket. This keeps her working even when she can't see the reward, which builds a stronger, more reliable behavior. Keep sessions short — 3 to 5 reps and done. End before she gets bored.
Place ▾
Pick a specific mat, bed, or flat surface and use it consistently — Place always means that spot. Step 1 — Get her on it: Lure her onto the mat with a treat. The moment all four paws are on it → "Yes!" and reward. Do this until she's stepping on it confidently. Step 2 — Build duration: Once she's on the mat, wait a beat before marking. Gradually stretch the time between her landing and the reward. Introduce a release word ("free" or "ok") — she stays until she hears it, even if you step away. Step 3 — Add distance: Once she'll hold Place with you standing next to her, take one step back. Mark and reward. Add distance slowly over multiple sessions. Step 4 — Send her: Eventually you can point to the mat and say "Place" from across the room and she'll go to it and wait. The release word is the whole game. She has to learn that getting up early means the reward disappears. Calmly return her to the mat without a fuss, reset, and try again.
Crate ▾
Say "Crate" and gesture toward the open door. If they hesitate, toss a treat inside. Once they walk in → "Yes!" and close the door calmly. No big fuss in either direction. If they whine: do not open the door. Wait for any pause — even two seconds of quiet — then open. Opening during whining teaches them that whining works. The crate is not punishment. Keep it matter-of-fact and routine. Dogs who learn to settle in a crate are safer, calmer, and easier to manage everywhere. For Luna specifically — she is actively teething and needs something appropriate to chew every time she's in the crate. Give her one or two of the following: • A Kong stuffed with her kibble and a few treats • A high-value treat like a pupperoni • A rubber or bone chewing stick • A frozen Kong stuffed with food, treats, and toppers These channel her need to bite toward the right thing while her adult teeth are coming in. When she takes it and starts chewing: praise her calmly once, then leave her alone. Don't hover — the goal is for her to settle and self-soothe. If you need to leave her for a while, a frozen Kong is the best option. It takes longer to work through and keeps her busy and calm without needing you nearby.
Loose Leash Walking ▾
Pick one side (left or right) and keep them there consistently. The rule: a tight leash means you stop. A loose leash means you move and reward. The moment the leash goes slack → "Yes!" and keep going. Reward while walking, not after you stop. If they pull: stop completely. Don't say anything. Don't yank. Just be a tree. When they turn back and the leash softens → mark and walk again. Keep sessions short. Five minutes of actual loose-leash walking does more than 20 minutes of managed pulling.
Enforcing the Leash (Without Burning Out) ▾
You don't need to fight the dog to enforce leash manners. Stop → wait → reward the loose leash → go again. That's the whole method. Repeat as needed, without frustration, without drama. Don't pop the leash or use it to yank — that teaches the dog to brace against pressure, not to walk nicely. If you're genuinely tired or frustrated, cut the walk short. A short calm walk is more valuable than a long tense one. The dog is not winning if you go home early — they're just not getting rewarded for bad behavior. Leash manners build over days and weeks, not a single session. Progress compounds quietly. Stay consistent and trust it.
Engagement Games
Name Game ▾
Say her name once, clearly and upbeat. The instant she looks at you → "Yes!" and a treat. That's it. The whole game is name → eye contact → jackpot. Start at close range with no distractions. As she gets reliable, play it in busier spots — the yard, on a walk, near other people or dogs. Every time she responds to her name in a distracting environment, reward bigger than usual. The rule: if she doesn't respond, don't repeat her name. Saying it twice teaches her that the first time is optional. Instead, make a noise (a kiss sound, a hand clap), let her find you, then reset and try again. A dog who snaps her head toward you the moment she hears her name is a dog you can reach anywhere. This game is the foundation for recall, focus, and everything else.

Novel Activity Day

How to prepare a dog for a bath, vet visit, nail trim, or grooming session

The sequence matters as much as the individual steps. A dog can't fully decompress unless their physical and mental needs are met first. Meet needs → decompress → then introduce mild stressors. Budget 75–100 minutes before the activity begins.
The Routine
1
Engagement & Games 5–15 min
Tug of war Fetch Sit / Down Paw Engagement
Burns the edge off without exhausting. Active play + a few commands gets their brain working and their body moving — but stops well before they're tired. This step is about arousal management, not fatigue. A dog that goes straight from resting to a novel stressor will be in a heightened state. A dog that's had a short, fun play session enters decompression from a calmer starting point.
2
Outdoor Play & Potty 20–30 min
Free sniff time Outdoor play Potty break
Let them sniff freely — sniffing actively lowers cortisol and shifts the nervous system toward parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) mode. This is not wasted time. The potty break also matters: a dog in a sympathetic/alert state often can't fully relax enough to eliminate. Getting a clean potty out before crating means fewer accidents and a more settled crate experience.
3
Crate + Frozen Kong 20–30 min
Frozen Kong Crate rest Door closed
Rhythmic licking activates the parasympathetic nervous system directly. A frozen Kong keeps them working at a slow, steady pace — it's a built-in decompression tool. Let them finish the Kong fully before moving to the next step. Don't rush this phase. The Kong is doing real physiological work, not just keeping them busy.
4
Nap / Full Settle ~15 min
Lying down relaxed Slow breathing Soft body language
Wait for the green-light signals — not a timer: • Lying down relaxed (not curled tight) • Slow, even breathing • Soft body language — no tension in the face or limbs • No fidgeting or position changes This is the signal that the nervous system has genuinely settled. Starting the novel activity before these signals appear is starting from the wrong baseline. The activity will go worse and build weaker associations.
5
Novel Activity 5–15 min prep
Bath Vet visit Nail trim Grooming
Starting from a calm, settled baseline means: • Less physical resistance during the activity • Lower cortisol = better emotional processing • The experience gets encoded as "manageable" rather than "overwhelming" • Better long-term associations with the activity build over time Each repetition of this routine compounds. Dogs that go through this preparation consistently become easier to bathe, trim, and groom as months pass — not because they're trained for those things specifically, but because their nervous system has learned that these events follow rest, not chaos.
Core Principle
Meet needs → Decompress → Then introduce mild stressors. The sequence creates the conditions for success. Individual steps alone don't — the order is what makes it work.

Reading Your Dog

Recognizing comfort, curiosity, play — and the full stress ladder

Dogs communicate discomfort on a progressive ladder, not all at once. Most bites that feel like they came out of nowhere had 4 earlier warning steps that went unnoticed or ignored. Dogs learn to skip soft signals when those signals haven't worked for them.
Green Signals
Relaxed & Neutral ▾
Loose, wiggly body · soft blinking eyes · relaxed ears (not pinned or fully forward) · mouth slightly open or softly closed · tail in neutral position with a gentle wag This is baseline comfort — the dog feels safe and has no agenda. It's the state you're aiming to return to after any stressful moment.
Playful & Inviting ▾
Play bow (front legs down, rear up) · bouncy exaggerated movements · high wagging tail · pawing at you or the air · zoomies after a good moment The dog is actively inviting interaction. Play bow is one of the clearest signals in the canine vocabulary — it means "what comes next is play, not a threat."
Curious & Alert ▾
Forward ears · head tilt · direct but soft eye contact · sniffing toward something · following you with eyes or body Alert without tension — exploring, not threatened. The key distinction from stress-alert is the softness of the eyes and the absence of stiffness in the body.
Calm & Trusting ▾
Full-body relaxation · belly exposure (in a safe, familiar context) · slow blinking · resting chin on your lap · sighing when settling The dog has chosen to be still near you. Belly exposure is context-dependent — in a relaxed home setting with someone trusted, it's vulnerability and trust. In a novel or stressful setting, it can be appeasement. Read the rest of the body.
The Ladder
Level 1
Soft Signals
▾
Yawning · lip licking · looking away · turning the body away How to respond: Ease up, lower your criteria, shorten the session. These are early and clear — honor them. A dog who shows soft signals is in a healthy relationship. They feel safe enough to communicate before escalating.
Level 2
Medium Signals
▾
Whale eye (whites of eyes visible) · ears flat · tail tucked · freezing or sudden stillness How to respond: Stop the exercise entirely. Give space. Let the dog decompress. The nervous system is signaling it's been pushed past comfort — don't add more pressure.
Level 3
Hard Signals
▾
Hard unblinking stare · stiff rigid body · low growl How to respond: Disengage completely. No punishment, no scolding the growl. A growl is communication — removing it without addressing the underlying state just eliminates a warning without fixing anything. End it calmly and give space.
Level 4
Warning Signals
▾
Snapping · air bite · showing teeth How to respond: Disengage completely and immediately. The dog escalated here because earlier signals were ignored or ineffective. After the moment passes, review: what was happening at Level 1 and 2 that got missed?
Level 5
Contact
▾
Bite contact. This almost never comes without steps 1–4 preceding it. If it felt like it came out of nowhere, the earlier signals were missed — not absent. After any bite incident: give full space, assess for injury, consult a trainer. No punishment after the fact — it increases anxiety without teaching a replacement behavior.
The Trust Principle
The goal is not a dog who never shows discomfort. It's a dog who expresses it early and softly because they trust you'll respond. Every time you honor an early signal — ease up, shorten the session, give space — your dog learns they don't need to escalate. The ladder gets used at the bottom, not the top. Note: A dog who vocalizes while obeying a command is expressing an opinion, not defiance. Watch for ladder signals, not vocalization alone.
